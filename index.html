A data story in four bars

Seven seconds

In 1969 a drummer played four bars nobody noticed. They became the most sampled drum break in music history, hiding in thousands of songs, probably including some you love.

Sound on · headphones help · tap the record to poke it

New here? A “break” is the moment a band drops out and only the drums play. A “sample” is a slice of an old record reused in a new song.

Before we start

You’ve probably heard it

This one drum pattern hides in thousands of songs. Press play and listen to the drums.

    Listen for a snappy “boom, boom-chick” and a rattling cymbal.

    1969

    A B-side nobody noticed

    The Winstons recorded “Amen, Brother” as a B-side. At 1:26 everyone else stops and drummer Gregory Coleman plays four bars alone. That’s the break.

    #1most-sampled track on WhoSampled

    Listen for the band dropping out, leaving only drums.

    About the audio and the song

    The drums you hear on this page are rebuilt in your browser from published transcriptions. They are not the 1969 recording, and the groove’s human timing is simplified. The 30-second Apple preview is the real track and may not reach the break. When a clip plays, the record looks for the break’s pattern in its drums and lights it up in time.

    The tune it’s built on

    Bars 1 & 2

    First, just funk

    For two bars Coleman plays a standard funk groove: kick, snare on 2 and 4, ride cymbal on every eighth note. Slow it down and it’s a hip-hop beat.

    Listen for a steady “boom, chick” with the cymbal ticking along. Nothing surprising yet.

    Tap a part to mute it. Tap the record to hear any slice.

    Bar 3

    The snare shows up late

    On beat four the snare slides back half a beat. Your ear expects a repeat and gets a stumble. Flip between what Coleman played and the beat you’d expect.

    Listen for the second “chick” arriving late, like a small stumble.

    dashed outline = where the snare “should” land

    Bar 4

    Then the downbeat vanishes

    No kick on the “one.” Instead: ghost snares tumble in, the kick doubles up, and an open cymbal splashes where the ride should be. The groove trips over itself, and that’s the hook.

    Listen for the “boom” you expect on the first beat never coming.

    1986

    DJs find it

    A DJ compilation, Ultimate Breaks and Beats, puts the break on a record made for looping, and Stetsasonic and Salt-N-Pepa sample it that year. Slow a record down and everything drops with it: a hip-hop drum kit.

    Listen for the same beat, lower and heavier.

      1988

      The year it escaped

      Mantronix chops it up until it’s the center of “King of the Beats.” N.W.A and Rob Base & DJ E-Z Rock use it in hits the same year. A drum break becomes a sound everyone knows without knowing why.

        1992 →

        Then Britain speeds it up

        Producers push it past 160 BPM and slice it into rolling, stuttering snares: jungle, then drum & bass. Everything gets higher and tighter, like a record on fast-forward.

        Listen for snares tumbling over each other.

          1991 – 1999

          Then the mainstream finds out

          By the 90s it has left the clubs. Primal Scream and The Prodigy use it, and in 1997 Bowie and Oasis both do. In 1999 it opens a cartoon set in the year 3000. More on that soon.

          Most of these hide the break under guitars and vocals, so there’s no clean clip to play. The next chapters have ones you can hear.

          2004 – 2012

          Everyone’s first chop

          So easy to cut up that it became a go-to for beat-makers. Breakcore shredded it to confetti. In 2004 Nate Harrison’s short film Can I Get an Amen? asked where it came from and who owns it.

            2020 → now

            It’s still going

            Fifty-seven years on, producers are still chopping it. In 2026 the Library of Congress added “Amen, Brother” to the National Recording Registry.

            Modern bass producers chop it so finely that a 30-second clip rarely shows the pattern, so there’s nothing to play here. The echoes chart below lists the ones that use it.

              Your turn

              Chop it like it’s 1993

              No music skills needed. Each pad is one beat of the break. Pick a slot, tap a pad, and rebuild the loop. Reorder the slices and the same four bars become a brand new groove, which is exactly how jungle producers worked in 1993.

                The drummer

                Who got paid?

                1. 1996

                  Richard Spencer, the bandleader who owned the copyright, first learns the break has been sampled — after the three-year statute of limitations has run out. He never receives royalties for it.

                2. 2006

                  Gregory Coleman, the drummer who played those four bars, dies homeless and destitute.

                3. 2015

                  Two British DJs raise more than £18,000 for Spencer through a GoFundMe campaign. He dies in 2020.

                4. 2026

                  The Library of Congress adds “Amen, Brother” to the National Recording Registry.
