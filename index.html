A data story in four bars

Seven seconds

In 1969 a drummer played four bars nobody noticed. Since then they’ve been borrowed by thousands of songs, probably including some you love.

Sound on · headphones help · tap the record to poke it

New here? A “break” is the moment a band drops out and only the drums play. A “sample” is a slice of an old record reused in a new song.

Before we start

You’ve probably heard it

This one drum pattern is hiding in thousands of songs. Press play on these three and listen to the drums.

    Listen for a snappy “boom, boom-chick” with a rattling cymbal. Hold on to that sound for the next minute.

    1969

    A B-side nobody noticed

    The Winstons cut “Amen, Brother” in about twenty minutes as the flip side of “Color Him Father,” the song that won the Grammy. Then, one minute and 26 seconds in, everyone else stops playing.

    Drummer Gregory Coleman plays four bars alone. That’s the break.

    Listen for the whole band dropping out so that only the drums are left.

    The tune it’s built on

    Bars 1 & 2

    First, just funk

    For two bars Coleman plays a standard funk groove: kick, snare on 2 and 4, ride cymbal on every eighth note. Slow it down and it’s a hip-hop beat.

    Listen for a steady “boom, chick” with the cymbal ticking along. Nothing surprising yet.

    Tap a part to mute it. Tap the record to hear any slice.

    Bar 3

    The snare shows up late

    On beat four the snare slides back half a beat. Your ear expects a repeat and gets a stumble. Flip between what Coleman played and the beat you’d expect.

    Listen for the second “chick” arriving late, like a small stumble.

    dashed outline = where the snare “should” land

    Bar 4

    Then the downbeat vanishes

    No kick on the “one.” Instead: ghost snares tumble in, the kick doubles up, and an open cymbal splashes where the ride should be. The groove trips over itself, and that’s the hook.

    Listen for the “boom” you expect on the first beat never coming.

    1986

    DJs find it

    A DJ compilation called Ultimate Breaks and Beats puts “Amen, Brother” on a record made for looping. That same year Stetsasonic and Salt-N-Pepa sample it.

    Slow a record down and everything drops with it: the tempo, the pitch, the weight. The break turns into a hip-hop drum kit.

    Listen for the same beat, lower and heavier, like a record slowed down.

      1988

      The year it escaped

      Mantronix chops it up until it’s the center of “King of the Beats.” N.W.A and Rob Base & DJ E-Z Rock use it in hits the same year. A drum break becomes a sound everyone knows without knowing why.

        1992 →

        Then Britain speeds it up

        Producers push the break past 160 BPM and slice it into rolling, stuttering snare patterns: jungle, then drum & bass. It’s easy to cut up, and that’s most of the story. Everything is higher and tighter now, just like a record on fast-forward.

        Listen for the beat rolling nearly twice as fast, with snares tumbling over each other.

            The drummer

            Who got paid?

            1. 1996

              Richard Spencer, the bandleader who owned the copyright, first learns the break has been sampled — after the three-year statute of limitations has run out. He never receives royalties for it.

            2. 2006

              Gregory Coleman, the drummer who played those four bars, dies homeless and destitute.

            3. 2015

              Two British DJs raise more than £18,000 for Spencer through a GoFundMe campaign. He dies in 2020.

            4. 2026

              The Library of Congress adds “Amen, Brother” to the National Recording Registry.
